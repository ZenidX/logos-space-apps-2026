## Part A — Iceland as a planetary analog

### A1. Apollo astronauts did geology field training in Iceland (1965 and 1967, Askja)

Primary source: W. C. Phinney, *Science Training History of the Apollo Astronauts*, NASA/SP-2015-626 (NASA Technical Reports Server).

- URL (PDF, 9.6 MB): https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190026783/downloads/20190026783.pdf — **200**
- Record page: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20190026783 — **200**
- Quote (main text): "Next was a trip to the Askja caldera of Iceland where there was beautiful volcanic geology with practically no vegetation cover (Figures 26 and 27). The features include calderas, ash cones, steaming volcanic vents, cinders, pumice, and various types of lava flows. Iceland was probably the most Moon-like of all the field areas that were visited."
- Quote (Appendix E, field-trip list, 1965 entry): "July 12-16, 1965 Iceland — Anders, Bassett, Bean, Cernan, Chaffee, Cunningham, Eisele, Schweikart, Scott, Williams. Beautiful volcanic geology with practically no vegetation cover. [...] Probably the most moon-like of the field areas. Leaders: Sigurdur Thorarinsson and Gudmundar Sigvaldson. [...] The geologic observations made by the astronauts around the edge of Askje Caldera produced a revision in the interpretation by Icelandic geologists of the origin of some of the units erupted from the caldera." (spellings "Schweikart", "Askje" as printed)
- Quote (Appendix E, 1967 entry): "July 2-8, 1967 Iceland — Anders, Armstrong, Brand, Carr, Duke, Engle, Evans, Garriot, Gibson, Haise, Irwin, Kerwin, Lind, Lousma, Mattingly, McCandless, Michel, Mitchell, Pogue, Roosa, Schmitt, Swigert, Weitz, Worden. [...] See field trip of July 12-16, 1965 for details."
- Dates/place stated: July 12-16, 1965 and July 2-8, 1967; Iceland, Askja caldera. Caveats: (a) the same document's chronological appendix lists the 1967 trip as "July 3-8, 1967 — 23 astronauts — Geology Field Trip to Iceland", i.e. a one-day discrepancy and 23 vs 24 names inside the document; (b) Askja is named explicitly for the 1965 trip; the 1967 entry only refers back to the 1965 entry for details.

Supporting NASA web page (current, no years given):
- https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/nasas-artemis-ii-crew-uses-iceland-terrain-for-lunar-training/ — **200**
- Quote: "Iceland has served as a lunar stand-in for training NASA astronauts since the days of the Apollo missions, and this summer the Artemis II crew took its place in that long history."
- Quote: "“Apollo astronauts said Iceland was one of the most lunar-like training locations that they went to in their training,” said Cindy Evans, Artemis geology training lead at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston."

Not found: a nasa.gov news/history article devoted to the 1965/1967 Iceland trips (several guessed URLs return 404; the nasa.gov site search returns none). A non-NASA page with the same dates is The Exploration Museum (Húsavík), https://www.explorationmuseum.com/astronaut-training/ — **200** ("...arrived in Iceland on July 12, 1965, and spent a week exploring the barren highlands and volcanic geology of Iceland"; "The astronauts visited Drekagil, Lake Mývatn, and Askja.") — museum page, not an agency source.

### A2. SAND-E rover/drone operations in Iceland, 2019

Primary source: Ewing et al., "Overview and Initial Results of SAND-E: Semi-Autonomous Navigation for Detrital Environments", 51st Lunar and Planetary Science Conference (2020), abstract 2857, hosted on the NASA Technical Reports Server (co-authors include NASA Johnson Space Center staff).

- URL (PDF): https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001815/downloads/20200001815.pdf — **200**
- Record page: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20200001815 — **200**
- Quote: "Using an Argo J5 rover instrumented with stereo cameras and Autonomous Soil Assessment System (ASAS) software, and an off the shelf quadcopter, SAND-E tested the use of automated terrain analysis and UAS data for science operations in a Mars-analog environment in Iceland during July of 2019."
- Quote (site): "We chose the Skjaldbreidauhraun glacial outwash plain, which sits at the base of Thórisjökull glacier. The site is 30 km north of Thingvellir National Park and ~2 hours from Reykjavik."
- Date/place stated: July 2019; Skjaldbreidauhraun glacial outwash plain, at the base of Thórisjökull glacier, Iceland.
- IMPORTANT: this NASA-hosted paper does **not** use the name "Lambahraun". No NASA or JPL web page naming Lambahraun was found (nasa.gov and science.nasa.gov site searches for "Lambahraun" return nothing; the mars.nasa.gov/JPL URLs tried are 404 or redirect to unrelated pages). The name "Lambahraun" appears only in press coverage:
  - Space.com (press, not agency): https://www.space.com/rovers-explore-iceland-lava-field-mars-2020.html — **200** — "At the foot of Langjokull, Iceland's second biggest glacier, on the black basalt sand of the Lambahraun lava field, scientists and engineers gathered for three weeks this summer to develop and test Martian rover and drone prototypes."
  - Recommendation: if the text must cite an official source, say "a glacial outwash plain at the base of Thórisjökull (July 2019)"; use "Lambahraun" only with the press attribution.
- Project partner page (not NASA): https://missioncontrolspace.com/stories/semi-autonomous-navigation-for-detrital-environments-sand-e/ — **200** — "Mission Control was the industry partner to SAND-E, a project led by Texas A&M University and funded by NASA PSTAR to study Mars-like volcanic sand environments in Iceland in advance of NASA’s Mars 2020 rover mission to the red planet." So SAND-E is NASA-funded (PSTAR) and led by Texas A&M University, not a NASA-run mission.

### A3. Rehearsing lunar-polar low-Sun lighting outdoors under a real low Sun

**None found.** The sources located describe the opposite approach: outdoor tests at night, with an artificial light standing in for the low Sun.

- USGS Astrogeology (JETT, San Francisco Volcanic Field, Arizona): https://www.usgs.gov/centers/astrogeology-science-center/news/artemis-iii-mission-tests-san-francisco-volcanic-field — **200** — "In order to simulate the challenging lighting conditions expected at the lunar South Pole, some of the JETT EVAs were planned to occur at night with a portable spotlight acting as the Sun, to simulate what it would be like to have the Sun low on the horizon." The page also states: "JETT5 conducted mission simulations for Artemis III from May 13 to 17, 2024."
- NASA D-RATS 2022 Test Report (NTRS PDF): https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230017073/downloads/D-RATS%202022%20Test%20Report%20Final.pdf — **200** — located via search, which reports that it simulated low-angle sunlight by testing at night with portable high-intensity lighting; the PDF text was not read line by line here, so no quote is given.
